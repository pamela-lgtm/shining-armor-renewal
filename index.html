Section Three

What supports the recommendation

The adjustment grid points to $2,350. We are recommending $2,375 — a difference of $25. Two independent approaches landing within about one percent of each other is the strongest confirmation available in a market this thin.

Two further pieces of evidence support the number.

The comparable appears to have been underpriced

409 Summit Ridge went under application with zero days on market — it was spoken for the day it listed. It also leased at $1,800 in 2023 and listed at exactly $1,800 again in 2026. Three years, no change.

A property that leases instantly is almost always priced below what the market would have paid. Anything we derive from it inherits that. The adjusted $2,350 is therefore more likely a floor than a ceiling.

Your actual rent is better evidence than any asking price

Your home has been renting at $2,295, paid reliably. That is not a number someone hoped for in a listing. It is a price the market has demonstrated it will pay for this specific home, for a full lease term.

A modest 3.5% increase on a proven, paid rent is a far sounder basis than a leap from a single dissimilar asking price.

So $2,375 is well supported. It sits essentially on top of the comparable analysis, and it is a modest step above a rent already being paid in a market where your resident has no comparable alternative.

Section Four

The balance we are weighing

This is the heart of the recommendation, and it is a judgment about risk rather than a calculation.

What the absence of competition means

If your resident decided to move, there is currently nothing comparable for them to move to in this zip code. No competing townhome. Nothing of this size and configuration. They would have to compromise — a smaller unit, a different product type, or a different area entirely — and pay moving costs to do it.

An $80 monthly increase costs them $960 over a year. A move costs them a new security deposit, application fees, movers, time away from work, and the disruption of relocating a household. The arithmetic does not favor leaving over $80, and a reasonable resident will see that.

What we are protecting

You have a responsible resident who pays on time and maintains the home. That is genuinely valuable and it is not easily replaced.

A single vacant month at $2,375 costs $2,375 — plus the turnover work, the marketing, and the leasing cost of placing someone new. The entire annual gain from this increase is $960.

Put plainly: losing this resident would cost more than two and a half years of the increase we are recommending. A twelve-month lease does not give you two and a half years.

That arithmetic is exactly why we are recommending a modest increase rather than an aggressive one. The increase is worth taking only because the probability of losing the resident over it is genuinely low. If that probability were higher, the right answer would be to hold flat.

Section Six

Our recommendation, and the decision

We recommend renewing at $2,375 for a twelve-month term.

The reasoning in short: there is no comparable competition in this market, which supports an increase. Your resident has nowhere comparable to go, which makes a modest increase low-risk. And vacancy is expensive enough that protecting a proven, reliable resident is worth more than the extra income a larger increase would produce.

It is a balance rather than a maximum. We are not trying to extract the highest number this home could theoretically command. We are trying to capture fair value while keeping a good resident in place, because over the life of your investment that combination produces more than an aggressive renewal followed by a vacancy.
